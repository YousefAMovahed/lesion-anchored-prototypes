\begin{abstract}
Melanoma classifiers pick up artifacts such as dark corners, rulers and ink, and a prototype network's explanation may point at them. On ISIC~2019, dark corners and bright structures remain associated with melanoma after stratifying by source. Existing remedies supervise accuracy or activations, but in dermoscopy none controls \emph{which} training patches prototypes may become. We propose lesion-anchored prototype learning. A lesion-attraction objective acts on each prototype's normalized spatial attention, and the projection step admits only cells that are at least half lesion and nearly free of detected artifacts. Every prototype thus comes from a lesion-dominated patch by construction, although its receptive field extends beyond it. Masks need no new annotation (Dice 0.94 on held-out expert masks); inference needs none. In a same-backbone, leakage-audited comparison, the model raises the AUC of ProtoPNet from 0.825 to 0.865 (0.859 without an auxiliary artifact-suppression term), also with the background masked. Swapped backgrounds sway it less than ProtoPNet or a CNN. With artifacts inserted around bit-identical lesion pixels, lesion attraction roughly halves ProtoPNet's decision-flip rate (dark corners: 10.9\%$\rightarrow$5.2\%). Both gains hold across seven runs per model ($p\leq0.03$), and ProtoPNet with the same projection stays less robust. The strongest mask-supervised ProtoPNet reaches similar accuracy (0.867) and, across runs, robustness, but put all prototypes on lesion-dominated cells in one of seven runs (ours: every run, at no accuracy cost) and learned more redundant prototypes. Adding that mask loss weakens the real dark-corner shortcut further; alone, it generalizes better to Derm7pt. Code and logs: \url{https://github.com/YousefAMovahed/lesion-anchored-prototypes}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Convolutional networks can classify skin lesions as accurately as dermatologists~\cite{esteva2017}, yet they also learn \emph{shortcuts}~\cite{geirhos2020}: features correlated with the label in the training data but unrelated to the disease. In dermoscopy, surgical skin markings raise the melanoma probability of a deep classifier~\cite{winkler2019}, models perform far above chance even when the lesion is removed~\cite{bissoto2019,bissoto2020}, and dark corners, rulers, gel bubbles and ink are common and unevenly spread across sites~\cite{bissoto2022,yan2023,pewton2022}.

Rudin~\cite{rudin2019} argues that clinical use calls for models interpretable by design. The prototypical part network (ProtoPNet)~\cite{chen2019} compares image patches with learned prototypes, each projected onto a real training patch, so a decision reads ``this part looks like that part''. Yet a prototype may be a piece of a ruler or a black corner; the shortcut then survives, presented as a reasonable explanation~\cite{hoffmann2021}, as seen in dermoscopy~\cite{correia2023,nauta2023pip}. \textbf{Research gap.} Three gaps remain (Table~\ref{tab:gap}). First, dermoscopic debiasing methods~\cite{bevan2022,yan2023,pahde2023} improve robustness to artifacts but are black boxes. Second, in prototype networks, mask supervision penalizes activation outside annotated regions~\cite{barnett2021,correia2023}, and user feedback removes or deactivates unwanted prototypes~\cite{bontempelli2023,nauta2023pip}. None constrains the \emph{push} step, which decides which training patch a prototype finally \emph{is}; feedback helps only after a user spots the problem. Third, artifact insertion has tested the \emph{decisions} of classifiers~\cite{nauta2022,nauta2023pip,pahde2023}, but, to our knowledge, not whether a prototype network's \emph{explanations} stay on the lesion when artifacts are added or removed. \textbf{Contributions.} We address these gaps:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Lesion attraction on the normalized prototype attention.} An objective on each prototype's spatial attention pulls it toward the lesion without shrinking activations, needs no new annotation and drives the accuracy and robustness gains: without it, accuracy drops and flip rates under inserted dark corners, rulers and ink roughly double. The main model gains 4.0 AUC points over ProtoPNet (3.4 with the two core components alone), also with the background masked and across seven runs, and ProtoPNet with the same projection flips 1.8 times as often under dark corners. Across runs, accuracy and robustness are on par with the mask-supervised ProtoPNet of the closest prior work~\cite{correia2023}, whose artifact share varies far more between runs and whose prototypes are more redundant.
\item \textbf{Lesion- and artifact-constrained projection.} The push step admits only latent cells whose input patch is at least half lesion and at most 5\% detected artifact, so every prototype comes from one \emph{by construction}. Mask losses give no such guarantee (all 20 prototypes on such cells in one of 19 runs). In 12 networks projected both ways, it changes neither the AUC nor, consistently, flip rates or maps: it fixes which patch an explanation shows and barely affects decisions. Unlike a concurrent foreground-restricted projection~\cite{lin2026}, it excludes artifacts, is combined with lesion attraction and is tested against shortcuts.
\item \textbf{A counterfactual test of decisions \emph{and} explanations.} We insert four artifact types and remove real hair and ruler marks with every lesion pixel bit-identical and measure how far decision and explanation move, with an inpainting-only null control, a test on unedited images, background masking and swaps and a detector-free prototype audit.
\item \textbf{A leakage-controlled, mask-audited evaluation.} We group near-duplicates in addition to lesion identifiers and validate every mask source and the artifact detectors. Eight configurations, four further mask-loss variants and their combination with our model share backbone and three seeds (PPN, the Correia formulation and Ours: seven) and are compared with lesion-grouped statistics, across training runs and on three external sets.
\end{itemize}
The main model also keeps an auxiliary artifact-suppression term; it brought no significant gain on its own, so we report it as a largely negative result rather than a contribution.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Artifacts and shortcuts in dermoscopy.} Bissoto \emph{et al.} annotated seven artifact types, found that simple debiasing does not remove their bias~\cite{bissoto2020} and built artifact-based generalization benchmarks~\cite{bissoto2022}. Removing dark corners barely changes accuracy but moves activations onto the lesion~\cite{pewton2022}, and pasted colored patches make a CNN call malignant lesions benign~\cite{nauta2022}. EPVT~\cite{yan2023} learns domain prompts for artifact groups, Bevan and Atapour-Abarghouei~\cite{bevan2022} unlearn artifact information adversarially, and Reveal-to-Revise (R2R)~\cite{pahde2023} finds band-aids, rulers and skin markers semi-automatically, corrects the model with RRR, CDEP or ClArC and tests it with pasted artifacts. All are black boxes, so we compare within prototype networks; they could complement anchoring.

\textbf{Prototype networks and explanation supervision.} The similarity a prototype network suggests can be misleading~\cite{hoffmann2021}, so explanations should be tested instead of taken at face value~\cite{adebayo2018}. RRR~\cite{ross2017} penalizes input gradients inside annotated irrelevant regions, and CDEP~\cite{rieger2020} penalizes contextual-decomposition importance, demonstrated on ISIC images with colored patches. IAIA-BL~\cite{barnett2021} adds a fine-annotation loss on prototype activation outside regions marked by radiologists. Closest to us, Correia \emph{et al.}~\cite{correia2023} apply this loss to melanoma-versus-nevus classification with lesion masks from a segmentation network; our PPN-FA baselines follow it, in its original and a weaker formulation (Section~\ref{sec:fa}). ProtoPDebug~\cite{bontempelli2023} and the medical study of PIP-Net~\cite{nauta2023pip} let a user mark confounded prototypes, which are then forgotten or switched off. Concurrently, Lin and Wright~\cite{lin2026} study architectural prototype diversity on birds and faces, with a hinge loss on the background share of each prototype's activation and, in an appendix, a projection restricted to patches with more than 50\% foreground.

\subsection{Prototypical part network}
Our network uses a DenseNet-121 backbone~\cite{huang2017} (Fig.~\ref{fig:masks}(b)). Two $1\times1$ convolutions and a sigmoid give a latent map $z\in[0,1]^{H\times W\times D}$, $H=W=7$, $D=128$. The $m=20$ prototypes $p_j\in\mathbb{R}^D$ are ten per class, $c(j)$ being the class of $p_j$. Each cell has distance $d_j(h,w)=\lVert z_{hw}-p_j\rVert_2^2$ and similarity $s_j(h,w)=\log\frac{d_j(h,w)+1}{d_j(h,w)+\epsilon}$ with $\epsilon=10^{-4}$. A prototype is as active as its best-matching cell, $a_j=\max_{h,w}s_j(h,w)$, and the logits are $\mathbf{W}\bm{a}$. Training minimizes
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}+\lambda_c\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{clst}}-\lambda_s\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sep}}+\lambda_l\Lles+\lambda_a\Lart ,
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}$ is a class-weighted cross-entropy and the cluster and separation costs measure the distance to the closest prototype of the own and of the other class~\cite{chen2019} ($\lambda_c=0.8$, $\lambda_s=0.08$ as originally). Our model combines its two core components, lesion attraction $\Lles$ ($\lambda_l=1$) and the constrained projection (Section~\ref{sec:push}), with an auxiliary artifact-suppression term $\Lart$ ($\lambda_a=1$; Section~\ref{sec:lart}), all using the masks of Section~\ref{sec:masks}.

\subsection{Lesion attraction (core component)}
Masks are augmented geometrically like the image, and average pooling to the $7\times7$ grid gives the lesion and artifact fractions of each cell, $M^L$ and $M^A$. Where a prototype looks is given by its spatial attention $\alpha_j=\mathrm{softmax}_{h,w}(s_j/\tau)$ with $\tau=0.5$, a soft version of the max-pooling location behind $a_j$. Each prototype is weighted by $\omega_j=a_j/\sum_k a_k$, treated as a constant, so that the prototypes the model uses count most. With $L_{ij}=\sum_{h,w}\alpha_{ij}M^L_i$, the attention mass of prototype $j$ on the lesion of image $i$,
\begin{equation}
\Lles=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}_L|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_L}\sum_{j:\,c(j)=y_i}\tilde{\omega}_{ij}\log\left(L_{ij}+\delta\right),
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{\omega}$ renormalizes $\omega$ over the prototypes of the true class $y_i$, $\delta=10^{-4}$, and $\mathcal{B}_L$ holds the batch images whose crop still shows lesion. The logarithm penalizes prototypes that almost ignore the lesion far more than those already on it. Since $\alpha_j$ sums to one, a larger lesion mass leaves less for everything else, artifacts included. The IAIA-BL fine-annotation loss~\cite{barnett2021,correia2023} penalizes activation magnitude outside a mask, whereas $\Lles$ acts on the normalized attention and leaves the activation scale to the other losses. Unlike the concurrent hinge on the background activation share~\cite{lin2026}, it rewards the lesion mass logarithmically and weights each prototype by its use.

\subsection{Lesion- and artifact-constrained projection (core component)}
\label{sec:push}
In the ``push'' step, ProtoPNet replaces each prototype with the closest latent patch from a training image of its class. This patch may lie on a ruler, a black corner or healthy skin even when the attention was well placed during training. We therefore admit only cells with $M^L\geq0.5$ and $M^A\leq0.05$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:masks}(b)). By construction, the ``that part'' of each explanation is then a $32\times32$ input patch that is at least half lesion and at most 5\% detected artifact. The constraint fixes where a prototype comes from, not every pixel that shapes its latent vector, since a cell also sees its surroundings (Section~\ref{sec:erf}). After the push, only $\mathbf{W}$ is trained (backbone, add-on layers and batch-normalization statistics fixed), so each prototype stays identical to its source patch. Five last-layer epochs did not solve this convex problem for \emph{any} prototype model, so we re-optimize $\mathbf{W}$ to convergence on fixed training activations (weighted cross-entropy plus an $\ell_1$ penalty of $10^{-4}$ on negative-class connections~\cite{chen2019}; 3{,}000 full-batch Adam steps, cosine-annealed learning rate from 0.02), identically for every prototype model and without validation data; we also report results without it.

\section{Masks: Provenance, Reliability and Leakage Control}
\label{sec:masks}
\subsection{Provenance and validation of the lesion masks}
Each image is tagged with its mask source. For the 10{,}015 HAM10000 images (39.5\% of ISIC~2019) we use the public expert segmentations~\cite{tschandl2018} unchanged. The 15{,}316 BCN20000~\cite{combalia2019} and MSK~\cite{codella2018} images have no public masks, so we predict them: we train a DeepLabV3~\cite{chen2017deeplab} network with an ImageNet-initialized MobileNetV3-Large~\cite{howard2019} encoder at $256\times256$ (12 epochs, AdamW, one-cycle learning rate up to $5\times10^{-4}$, binary cross-entropy plus soft Dice, random flips and $90^\circ$ rotations) \emph{only} on the 8{,}163 HAM10000 images of our (previously fixed) training split. Predictions are averaged with those of the flipped image, thresholded at 0.5, reduced to the largest component and hole-filled. Masks covering less than 0.5\% of the image (19 images, 0.08\%) are marked invalid and excluded from mask-based losses and metrics. On the 1{,}852 HAM10000 images of the validation and test splits, which the segmentation network never saw, predicted masks reach a mean Dice of 0.9445 against the expert masks (median 0.9672, mean Jaccard 0.9015), and on the 200 expert-segmented PH2 images~\cite{mendonca2013}, from another clinic and device, 0.918 without adaptation. Section~\ref{sec:maskrob} tests the dependence on mask quality.

\subsection{Artifact masks}
Artifact masks come from fixed, label-free image operations at $256\times256$ and always exclude the dilated lesion. \emph{Dark corners} (DC) are dark regions (HSV value $<50$) touching the border and at least 8~px thick on average. \emph{Thin dark structures} (TS: hair, ruler ticks) are found with a $13\times13$ black-hat filter as in DullRazor~\cite{lee1997} plus an elongation test, \emph{bright structures} (SP: specular highlights, gel-bubble rims, also light hairs) with an $11\times11$ white top-hat filter, and \emph{ink or colored patches} (INK) as strongly saturated blue-to-violet pixels (Fig.~\ref{fig:masks}(a)). An image is flagged once a mask covers more than 1\%, 0.3\%, 0.05\% or 0.1\% of it. Against the public manual labels of Bissoto \emph{et al.}~\cite{bissoto2020} for 2{,}593 of our MSK images, the flags are precise but conservative (precision/recall: DC vs.\ dark corner 0.92/0.10, TS vs.\ hair or ruler 0.96/0.62, SP vs.\ gel bubble or border 0.86/0.35, INK vs.\ ink or patches 0.74/0.25); a visual check of 24 random DC and 24 random TS flags agrees (24 and 20 correct). Many artifacts are missed, above all faint dark corners (37\% vs.\ 4\% of these images). ``Without artifacts'' therefore means ``without \emph{detected} artifacts'': an undetected artifact can enter a prototype cell, artifact shares count detected ones only, and the reference group of Section~\ref{sec:real} contains undetected ones, pulling $A$ toward 0.5 for every model. In the 2{,}092 manually labeled training images, only 1.9\% of the admissible cells lie on the outer ring of an image with an undetected dark corner. For hair or rulers, gel and ink, which the labels do not localize, only loose upper bounds exist (40\%, 43\%, 8\%). None of the 5 prototypes of Ours from these images is such a cell.

\subsection{How masks are used, and why they cannot leak}
Masks serve only the training losses, the constrained projection, the attribution metrics and the placement of edits; the classifier never receives one, so test predictions do not depend on test masks. The split was computed first, from identifiers and image content alone, before any model was trained. Grouping by lesion identifier and near-duplicate pairs (Section~\ref{sec:data}) can only remove leakage; an audit found no group, lesion identifier or image shared between sets, and all 342 near-identical pairs (thumbnail correlation $\geq0.97$) and other verified near-duplicate pairs lie within one set. Supervision uses training data only (segmentation network, mask-based losses, projection, last-layer re-optimization). Thresholds and checkpoints are chosen on validation, and the 1{,}852 expert masks of validation and test serve only for evaluation. Neither the segmentation network nor the artifact detectors see a diagnosis, so the masks cannot carry label information. Derm7pt, PAD-UFES-20 and PH2 are used for evaluation only; removing external images flagged as possible near-duplicates of ISIC training images (8, 6, 7) changes no external AUC by over 0.004.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\subsection{Data and leakage-free split}
\label{sec:data}
The task is melanoma versus all other diagnoses on the 25{,}331 training images of ISIC~2019 (HAM10000~\cite{tschandl2018}, BCN20000~\cite{combalia2019}, MSK~\cite{codella2018}; 4{,}522 melanomas, 17.9\%), resized to $256\times256$. Unlike common practice, we keep the 2{,}084 MSK images without a lesion identifier. To avoid leakage, we group images by lesion identifier and by near-duplicate pairs (perceptual-hash distance $\leq8$ and thumbnail correlation $\geq0.9$ for full and central image). The 13{,}259 groups are split into ten stratified folds, one for testing and one for validation (about 81/10/9\% per source), with almost identical composition (Table~\ref{tab:dist}). \subsection{Models and training}
All configurations share backbone (ImageNet-pretrained DenseNet-121), input size ($224\times224$), data, split and schedule. \textbf{CNN}: backbone, global average pooling, linear layer (black box). \textbf{PPN-std}: ProtoPNet with standard augmentation (random crop, horizontal flip). \textbf{PPN}: ProtoPNet with strong augmentation (random resized crop, scale $[0.4,1]$; horizontal and vertical flips; $\pm20\%$ brightness, contrast, saturation), used by all following models. \textbf{PPN-FA}: PPN with the IAIA-BL fine-annotation loss~\cite{barnett2021} on our lesion masks as in Correia \emph{et al.}~\cite{correia2023}, with their $\lambda=10^{-3}$ but computed on the $7\times7$ maps and averaged over the batch, which weakens it (Section~\ref{sec:fa} tests the original formulations and larger weights). \textbf{Ours}: lesion attraction, constrained projection and the auxiliary $\Lart$ ($\lambda_l=\lambda_a=1$ in Eq.~(\ref{eq:loss})). \textbf{Ablations}: without $\Lles$ (Ours-noLes), without $\Lart$ (Ours-noArt, the two core components only), with the standard projection (Ours-stdPush).

We train with Adam, batch size 64 and mixed precision: 5 epochs with frozen backbone (add-on layers and prototypes at $3\times10^{-3}$), 15 epochs of all layers (backbone $10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-3}$, learning rate divided by 10 every 5 epochs), then the projection and 5 last-layer epochs. The CNN trains for the same 20 epochs and keeps its best validation checkpoint. Class weights are 0.608 and 2.81. Prototype models are always evaluated after the projection, with the last-layer epoch chosen on validation. Each configuration runs with seeds 0, 1 and 2 (about 30 min per run on an NVIDIA T4); PPN, Correia (Section~\ref{sec:fa}) and Ours also have seeds 3--6, with trained prototypes stored before the projection. Images and masks, stored at $256\times256$, receive the same random crop and flips before bilinear resampling to the $224\times224$ input; test images are only resized. After pooling to the $7\times7$ grid, each cell covers a $32\times32$ input patch, and attribution maps are compared with masks at $224\times224$. Loss weights and the eight configurations, including the choice of the main model, were fixed before any test evaluation; later variants and seeds changed none of them.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
\textbf{Classification.} Each run's threshold is set on ISIC validation (Youden index) and reused for every test set. AUC, balanced accuracy (BAcc), sensitivity and specificity are mean $\pm$ std over seeds; tests use seed ensembles (mean probability).

\textbf{Attribution.} For a prototype model, we upsample the similarity maps of the three most activated prototypes to $224\times224$; their top 5\% of pixels (the 95th-percentile region that ProtoPNet visualizes) form the hot region. For the CNN we use the class-activation map, which with a global-average-pooling head equals Grad-CAM~\cite{selvaraju2017} up to a positive factor~\cite{zhou2016}. We report the share of the hot region on the lesion and on artifacts, whether its maximum falls on an artifact (pointing), and the \emph{enrichment}, i.e., artifact share divided by artifact area (above 1: more attention than the area explains).

\textbf{Counterfactuals.} Outside a 4-pixel margin around the lesion, we add a ruler along one border, 12--29 curved hairs, a dark vignette or 2--4 purple ink arcs to each test image. Conversely, detected thin dark structures (real hair and ruler marks, 544 images) are erased by Telea inpainting~\cite{telea2004} outside a 2-pixel margin. As a null control, a same-shaped region on artifact-free skin is inpainted. We verify that every lesion pixel stays bit-identical and record the mean $|\Delta p|$ of the melanoma probability, the rate of flipped decisions and, for the three prototypes most active on the original image, the Spearman correlation $\rho$ between the top-1 maps before and after the edit and the \emph{capture}, the new hot region's share on inserted pixels. Lesion-only images set all pixels beyond a 7-px rim around the lesion to the median color of a 10-px skin ring; background-only images Telea-inpaint the dilated lesion; background swaps~\cite{xiao2021} paste each lesion, pixel-identical and blended across its rim, onto the background-only image of another lesion of the same, a random or the opposite class.

\textbf{Statistics.} The 2{,}320 test images form 1{,}234 groups (a lesion with its near-duplicates; 471 with several images), so we resample groups: ensemble AUCs get 95\% CIs from 1{,}000 group resamples and are compared by a paired bootstrap with 2{,}000 (two-sided $p$), per-image metrics (averaged over seeds, then within groups) by the paired Wilcoxon test. Derm7pt (one image per case) uses the paired DeLong test~\cite{delong1988,sun2014}, PAD-UFES-20 a bootstrap over lesions. The Holm-corrected primary family holds the 16 main-claim tests: Ours against each of PPN-std, PPN, PPN-FA and the strongest mask-loss variant of Section~\ref{sec:fa} on AUC, artifact share and flip rate under dark corners and rulers. All other tests are secondary and uncorrected; of the 12 main background, diversity and coverage comparisons, 10 survive Holm correction (not lesion-only ensembles vs.\ PPN-FA, IAIA-BL: $p=0.06$). \subsection{Classification}
\label{sec:cls}
On ISIC test, Ours has the highest AUC of the prototype models (Table~\ref{tab:cls}). Its ensemble exceeds those of PPN-std, PPN and PPN-FA by 0.022, 0.018 and 0.017 (95\% CI $[0.009,0.036]$, $[0.004,0.032]$, $[0.005,0.031]$; Holm-adjusted $p=0.012$, $0.042$, $0.029$), and its seeds vary less. Each of the three Ours seeds beats each of the 13 seeds of PPN-std, PPN (seven) and PPN-FA (worst 0.8592 vs.\ best 0.8526; Derm7pt-dermoscopic 0.7405 vs.\ 0.7343). One of seven Ours seeds (0.863$\pm$0.012, range 0.842--0.880) falls below the best baseline seed; across runs, Ours exceeds these 13 seeds and the seven PPN seeds (0.821$\pm$0.039; exact Mann--Whitney $p=0.001$, $0.007$). At the validation threshold, Ours trades sensitivity for specificity; at 90\% sensitivity (test ROC), its specificity is highest among prototype models (64.8\%; PPN-std, PPN, PPN-FA 58.9--60.2\%, Correia 63.3\%, CNN 69.6\%; ensembles $p\geq0.14$; over seven runs vs.\ PPN $p=0.025$, vs.\ Correia $p=0.90$), and a validation threshold for 90\% sensitivity reaches 92.6\% on test. The black-box CNN remains more accurate ($p=0.009$), a price of about 3 AUC points for interpretability (4.6 for PPN) that vanishes on swapped backgrounds. With only the five last-layer epochs, all prototype models score lower, Ours least (0.019 vs.\ 0.039--0.079), and it stays best (0.846$\pm$0.007; baselines 0.765--0.797, ablations 0.787--0.841; $p\leq0.001$ against the baselines).


\textbf{Lesion versus background.} On lesion-only images, the Ours ensemble keeps its lead over PPN-std, PPN, PPN-FA and IAIA-BL (0.874 vs.\ 0.850--0.859, $p\leq0.03$; Correia 0.878, n.s.), and its seven runs exceed all 13 ProtoPNet runs and those of Ours-noLes ($p=3\times10^{-5}$, $0.017$); Ours and the prototype baselines lose at most 0.011 AUC, the CNN 0.024. On background-only images, the ensembles still reach 0.61--0.67 (Ours 0.635, $p\geq0.15$; across runs PPN scatters around chance, 0.44--0.66, $p=0.011$), which lesion size alone, still visible as the filled region, matches (0.67). In background swaps, the BG-Gap of the Ours ensemble is about half that of PPN-std, PPN, PPN-FA, the CNN and Ours-noLes (0.023 vs.\ 0.039--0.059, $p\leq0.002$; across runs $p=0.015$ vs.\ 13 ProtoPNet runs), on par with the mask losses (0.018, $p\geq0.22$). On random backgrounds Ours is as accurate as the CNN (mean over runs 0.834 vs.\ 0.826), on opposite-class ones nominally more (0.803 vs.\ 0.778, $p=0.067$), so part of the CNN's lead rests on the background.

Externally, Ours has the highest AUC of the main prototype models on both Derm7pt sets. On the dermoscopic images its ensemble beats PPN-FA and PPN-std ($p=1.7\times10^{-5}$, $2.8\times10^{-6}$, also after Holm correction) but not PPN ($p=0.20$), whose seeds vary strongly. On the clinical images, its advantage over PPN-std and PPN ($p=0.023$, $0.046$) does not survive Holm correction ($p=0.069$, $0.092$), and over PPN-FA is not significant ($p=0.30$). On PAD-UFES-20 (52 melanoma images of 36 lesions), Ours beats PPN-std ($p=0.001$) and does not differ significantly from PPN and PPN-FA ($p\geq0.23$); its mean AUC there is even below that of PPN. The black-box EPVT reports 0.837 on Derm7pt-dermoscopic~\cite{yan2023} (melanoma vs.\ benign, other split, ViT-B/16); our CNN reaches 0.799.

\subsection{Where the models look}
\label{sec:attr}
The black-box CNN is the model most drawn to artifacts (Table~\ref{tab:attr}, Fig.~\ref{fig:expl}(a)): in images with artifacts, 23.1\% of its hot region lies on them, 1.5 times their area share. The prototype models look at artifacts less than their area suggests, Ours least (4.8$\pm$1.5\% of the hot region, maximum on an artifact in 1.3$\pm$0.5\% of images; $\pm$: std over seeds), with the largest lesion fraction. In every Ours run, all 20 prototypes lie on lesion cells ($M^L\geq0.5$; Fig.~\ref{fig:erf}), whereas PPN-FA projects on average 10.0 onto lesion and 2.0 onto artifact cells. Most attention on artifacts falls on dark corners, where Ours has the smallest share. On the test sample, Ours differs significantly from each other main model in lesion and artifact share, enrichment and pointing ($p\leq5.6\times10^{-4}$; primary-family artifact shares: Holm-adjusted $p\leq2.2\times10^{-5}$). Across runs, they overlap with PPN and PPN-std (Section~\ref{sec:cf}). On Derm7pt-dermoscopic, it again has the lowest artifact share, enrichment and pointing ($p\leq8.6\times10^{-3}$), but the prototype baselines place more hot region on the lesion ($p\leq1.2\times10^{-4}$).

\textbf{Receptive field of the prototypes.}\label{sec:erf} The effective receptive field (ERF) of a prototype's source cell is the input-gradient magnitude of its latent vector (eight random projections). For every model, only 8--12\% of the mass lies inside the cell, and half spreads over 8--11\% of the image. For Ours, $69\pm3$\% lies on the lesion (10th percentile 42\%), vs.\ 48--53\% for PPN-std, PPN and PPN-FA (10th percentiles 11--21\%), and 2.5\% on detected artifacts (4.3\%, 2.7\%, 7.3\%). This reflects where prototypes are taken, not a narrower field: the constraint selects source images whose lesion covers 54\% of the image (baselines 40\%), and relative to lesion area every model concentrates its ERF on the lesion by a median factor of only 1.2--1.3. Ours-stdPush drops to 47\%; Ours-noLes, Ours-noArt keep 71--72\%. For 13\% of Ours prototypes (baselines 22--28\%), the ERF draws less on the lesion than its area would, so anchoring covers the projected cell and most, not all, of its receptive field.

\subsection{Counterfactual robustness}
\label{sec:cf}
Table~\ref{tab:cf} summarizes the interventions (2{,}063--2{,}318 images per insertion). Adding dark corners \emph{increases} the melanoma probability of every model (mean signed change $+0.041$ CNN, $+0.048$ PPN, $+0.020$ Ours), matching the association in Table~\ref{tab:bias}; the models have absorbed the shortcut, Ours the least. Ours roughly halves the flip rate of PPN for dark corners (10.9\%$\rightarrow$5.2\%) and rulers, reduces it for ink and hair and flips less than the CNN under all four (Table~\ref{tab:cf}). Against every other main model, all four reductions are significant ($p<10^{-6}$ vs.\ the prototype baselines, also Holm-adjusted for dark corners and rulers; $p\leq0.008$ vs.\ the CNN), as is the smaller $|\Delta p|$ vs.\ PPN ($p<10^{-6}$) and, except for hair ($p=0.09$), the CNN ($p\leq2.3\times10^{-4}$). Removing real hair and ruler marks flips more decisions than the null control for every model (Ours 4.4\% vs.\ 1.6\%), so inpainting cannot explain it.

\textbf{Threshold and direction.} At the validation threshold for 90\% sensitivity, Ours still flips less than every baseline under dark corners (3.7\% vs.\ 4.9--6.9\%; $p\leq0.004$), than the ProtoPNet baselines and the CNN under rulers (2.6\% vs.\ 4.1--7.1\%; $p\leq0.002$) and than PPN-std, PPN, IAIA-BL and the CNN under ink ($p\leq0.025$); under hair only than the CNN, and Correia flips fewer (9.8\% vs.\ 11.4\%, $p<10^{-4}$). At the Youden threshold, dark corners and rulers mostly flip benign to melanoma (PPN 9.7 of 10.9\%, 5.2 of 7.5\%), hair the reverse (Ours 7.7\%, PPN 7.8\%). Ours has fewer benign-to-melanoma flips than all prototype baselines under each insertion (corners, ruler, hair, ink: 4.1, 2.2, 1.5, 1.8\% vs.\ $\geq$4.8, 2.5, 3.2, 2.6\%).

The explanations of Ours also move the least (Fig.~\ref{fig:expl}(b)). Under added dark corners, its top-1 map keeps $\rho=0.92$ (PPN 0.68), and only 3.4\% of its hot region (per seed 0.2--7.7\%) moves onto them ($p<10^{-6}$ vs.\ PPN and PPN-FA). Ink, drawn just outside the lesion border where anchored prototypes respond, is the exception: 1.8\% of the hot region of Ours falls on it vs.\ 1.0\% for PPN, though it still flips less.

Each of the seven Ours seeds flips fewer decisions under dark corners and ink than 12 of the 13 seeds of PPN-std, PPN and PPN-FA (3.3--7.5\% vs.\ 7.6--15.4\%; 4.4--8.0\% vs.\ 8.0--11.2\%, i.e., at most 184 vs.\ at least 185 of 2{,}301 images); the exception is the lowest-AUC PPN seed (0.752; 2.0\%, 6.4\%). Across runs, Ours flips less than these 13 seeds under all four insertions ($p\leq0.004$) and the seven PPN seeds ($p\leq0.026$), not the seven Correia seeds ($p\geq0.62$). Every Ours seed also keeps a more stable map than any PPN-std or PPN-FA seed ($\rho$ 0.85--0.93 vs.\ $\leq$0.77); $\rho$ overlaps with two PPN seeds (0.87) and the mask-loss variants ($\leq$0.90). The artifact share (3.0--8.9\%) lies below every CNN seed ($\geq$20.7\%) but overlaps with the prototype baselines and Correia, and the captured share overlaps for all (Ours 0.2--7.7\%, baselines 0.1--15.4\%).

\textbf{Prototypes without detectors.} Under insertions, the prototypes of Ours change their mean activation ($d$, in between-image SDs) as much as those of PPN ($p\geq0.62$), so their stability does not come from inert prototypes. No baseline, IAIA-BL or Correia prototype reaches $d=0.27$: flips stem from many small shifts set by training (Table~\ref{tab:fact}); single prototypes of Ours rise more ($d\leq0.84$; $>0.5$ under hair in four runs, dark corners in one), yet its decisions flip less. Ours also has the least redundant prototypes: over the test images, their activations correlate less (mean absolute Spearman $r$ 0.71 vs.\ 0.83--0.88), and the participation ratio $(\sum_k\lambda_k)^2/\sum_k\lambda_k^2$ of the correlation matrix's eigenvalues $\lambda_k$ (PR, the effective number of distinct prototypes) is higher in every Ours run than in any baseline, IAIA-BL or Correia run (1.63--1.97 vs.\ 1.11--1.62; $p=6\times10^{-4}$ vs.\ PPN, Correia), mainly among benign prototypes, owing to lesion attraction (Ours-noLes 1.41, Ours-stdPush 1.79), although one factor dominates in every model. The benign prototypes of Ours also cover the benign diagnoses best: averaged over the seven benign diagnoses, the best one-vs-rest AUC of a benign prototype is 0.617 vs.\ 0.540--0.583 ($p=0.002$ vs.\ PPN and Correia), although none reaches 0.75. On Derm7pt, Correia aligns more prototypes with seven-point criteria (within-diagnosis AUC $\geq$0.60; 19 vs.\ 13, $p=0.001$), but in fewer distinct groups ($r>0.9$; 5.3 vs.\ 9.4, $p=0.054$); all align weakly (mean best AUC 0.57--0.60).

\subsection{Shortcuts in unedited images}
\label{sec:real}
Without editing, we ask whether test-image artifacts raise the melanoma score of \emph{benign} lesions (threshold-free $A$ of Table~\ref{tab:real}; 0.5: no effect). On ISIC, dark corners double the false-positive rate of PPN-FA but barely raise that of Ours. The effect $A$ of Ours is significantly smaller than that of PPN, PPN-FA and Ours-noLes ($p<0.001$) and does not differ from that of PPN-std and the CNN ($p\geq0.77$). Lesion attraction thus keeps the real dark-corner shortcut small, our weak mask loss leaves it in place, and the original formulations also reduce it (Section~\ref{sec:fa}). The same holds for bright structures, where Ours has the smallest effect of the prototype models ($p\leq0.038$ against PPN-std and PPN-FA), and most clearly for dark borders in clinical Derm7pt images (DeLong $p\leq1.4\times10^{-6}$). The CNN attends to dark corners most but has no larger effect.

\subsection{Ablation of the two core components}
\label{sec:abl}
\textbf{Lesion attraction} drives the accuracy gain. Without it (Ours-noLes), the test AUC falls to 0.830 ($p<0.001$), the artifact share more than doubles (4.8\%$\rightarrow$10.6\%), and dark-corner, ruler and ink flip rates roughly double. Ours-noLes keeps the constrained projection and all prototypes on lesion cells; a correct projection does not suffice without anchored attention.

\textbf{The constrained projection} determines what the prototypes \emph{are} (L-pr.\ in Table~\ref{tab:attr}). It does not change the accuracy (ensemble difference within $[-0.006,0.005]$, 95\% CI) or, significantly, the flip rates ($p\geq0.13$), and the differences of its maps in Tables~\ref{tab:attr} and~\ref{tab:cf} are not systematic. In a factorial control (Table~\ref{tab:fact}), we projected each network of seeds 3--6 of PPN, Correia and Ours both ways from its stored prototypes, refitting the last layer. In all 12 networks it put the 20 prototypes on lesion cells (before 6--17, one Correia network 20; mean lesion fraction 0.62$\rightarrow$0.90; exact Wilcoxon $p=0.001$) and changed no measure of accuracy, attribution or robustness consistently (AUC $+0.0001$; others $p\geq0.067$, both signs); seeds 0 and 2 of Ours and Ours-stdPush, which share their trained network (seed 1 diverged in training), agree. With the same constrained projection, Ours keeps a higher ensemble AUC than PPN (0.886 vs.\ 0.866, $p=0.001$), less artifact share ($p<10^{-5}$) and fewer flips under all four insertions ($p\leq4\times10^{-4}$), as with the standard projection: robustness comes from lesion attraction, not the projection.

\subsection{Strength of the mask-loss baseline}
\label{sec:fa}
IAIA-BL~\cite{barnett2021} and Correia \emph{et al.}~\cite{correia2023} compute the fine-annotation loss at $224\times224$ and sum it over the batch; with the same $\lambda=10^{-3}$, it is about three orders of magnitude stronger than in PPN-FA (factors 32 and 64; Correia \emph{et al.} penalize own-class prototypes only). We trained both original formulations and PPN-FA with $\lambda=10^{-2}$ and $10^{-1}$, three seeds each (Table~\ref{tab:fa}; secondary). Correia's formulation does not differ significantly from Ours in ISIC AUC ($-0.001$, 95\% CI $[-0.012,0.011]$) and generalizes better (Derm7pt-dermoscopic, -clinical, PAD-UFES-20: 0.771, 0.702, 0.699 vs.\ 0.749, 0.657, 0.647; $p\leq0.028$), but puts fewer prototypes on lesion cells and three times more hot region on artifacts ($p<10^{-6}$). In the primary family, Ours is significantly better on artifact share and dark-corner flips (Holm $p<10^{-6}$, $p=0.029$), not on AUC or ruler flips. Neither advantage replicates on seeds 3--6: there, Correia puts 13--20 prototypes on lesion cells, has less artifact share (4.2\% vs.\ 5.2\%, $p<0.001$) and flips as often ($p\geq0.17$), while less of the hot region of Ours moves onto inserted dark corners (1.0--1.9\% vs.\ 3.8--4.4\%, $p<10^{-5}$); it again generalizes better to Derm7pt-clinical (0.716 vs.\ 0.689, $p\leq0.022$). Over seven seeds, its artifact share spans 0.5--24.6\% (Ours 3.0--8.9\%), and neither AUC nor flip rates differ across runs ($p\geq0.62$). IAIA-BL keeps its hot region on the lesion (83\%) and off artifacts better than Ours ($p<0.002$) but is less accurate (ensembles $p=0.057$) and leaves prototypes off lesion cells. On seeds 0--2, every variant flips more than Ours under dark corners and ink (6.6--10.7\% vs.\ 5.6\%; $p\leq0.02$), though per seed the original formulations, and for dark corners $\lambda=10^{-1}$, overlap with Ours; under rulers the original formulations ($p\geq0.052$) and under hair Correia ($p=0.37$) do not differ significantly, and none keeps its explanation as stable. The original formulations' real dark-corner effect does not differ significantly from that of Ours ($p\geq0.26$), unlike our weak version. Mask supervision thus shapes where the network looks and helps it generalize; anchoring matches its robustness, fixes what the prototypes are and keeps them diverse and the artifact share stable across runs; the two are complementary.

Adding the Correia loss to Ours (Ours+Correia) gives the highest ISIC AUC of all prototype models ($p\geq0.32$ against Ours and Correia), maps more lesion-focused than Ours (lesion share 73\% vs.\ 59\%, seeds 59--85\% vs.\ 49--69\%; captured share 2.2\% vs.\ 3.4\%, less artifact share; $p<10^{-6}$), the smallest real dark-corner effect of all models ($p<0.001$ vs.\ both) and similar flip rates ($p\geq0.26$; hair 7.9\% vs.\ 9.2\%, $p=0.02$). It does not inherit the Derm7pt advantage of the mask loss (0.736 and 0.675 vs.\ 0.771 and 0.702; DeLong $p\leq0.016$), although its maps are more lesion-focused than Correia's (73\% vs.\ 60\%), so lesion focus alone cannot explain it; it has the highest PAD-UFES-20 AUC of all models (0.737, CNN 0.721; $p\leq0.001$ against both) and less stable maps than Ours.

\subsection{Artifact suppression: a largely negative result}
\label{sec:lart}
The auxiliary term penalizes attention on artifacts directly: with $A_{ij}=\sum_{h,w}\alpha_{ij}M^A_i$, the attention mass of prototype $j$ on the artifacts of image $i$, $\Lart=|\mathcal{B}|^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\omega_{ij}A_{ij}$. Removing it (Ours-noArt) changes accuracy significantly neither on ISIC (ensemble difference 0.007, 95\% CI $[-0.004,0.018]$) nor on any external set ($p\geq0.058$). Several counterfactual measures are even better without it (e.g., $|\Delta p|$ 0.061 vs.\ 0.081 for added hair, $p<10^{-6}$), and the attribution changes little (artifact share $p=0.36$; pointing $p=5.7\times10^{-5}$; Table~\ref{tab:attr}). The explanation gains come from the two core components alone: against PPN-std, PPN and PPN-FA, Ours-noArt has more hot region on the lesion, less on artifacts and fewer flips under all four insertions ($p\leq5.2\times10^{-4}$). Its AUC gain over them (0.010--0.016) is not significant with lesion groups resampled ($p=0.073$--$0.17$; image-level DeLong $p=0.005$ against PPN-std), so the significant accuracy gain belongs to the main model. $\Lart$ mainly weakens the response to real bright structures and to dark border regions in clinical images ($A$ 0.59 vs.\ 0.62 and 0.69 vs.\ 0.75; $p<0.001$, Table~\ref{tab:real}); otherwise its effect is small, so it remains auxiliary.

\subsection{Robustness to mask quality}
\label{sec:maskrob}
Predicted masks could bias the results if a model looked ``on the lesion'' only by an imperfect mask, so we split the ISIC test set by mask provenance and perturb the lesion masks (Table~\ref{tab:maskrob}). On the HAM10000 test images, which have expert masks, Ours places the most hot region on the lesion, a \emph{larger} advantage than on the whole test set (+8.8 vs.\ +2.7 points over the best baseline), so predicted-mask errors do not produce it; its artifact scores are the lowest there too. On the predicted-mask images, Ours has the lowest artifact share, although PPN places slightly more on the lesion (63.5$\pm$1.5\% vs.\ 62.4$\pm$2.6\%).

Eroding or dilating every lesion mask by 5~pixels shifts the lesion fractions of all models alike and keeps the order of the five main models (Ours first); artifact share, pointing and enrichment change by at most 0.011. The provenance guarantee itself depends on the mask border: with masks eroded by 5 pixels, on average 17 of the 20 prototypes of Ours remain on cells with $\geq$50\% lesion (lowest 0.36), all 20 with dilated masks. Ours is also the most accurate of the four main prototype models in every source, with expert (HAM10000) and with predicted training masks (BCN20000, MSK; Table~\ref{tab:maskrob}).

A too-small predicted mask could let inserted artifacts touch the lesion. On expert masks, where the protected region is exact, Ours flips the fewest decisions (dark corners 4.4\% vs.\ 7.1\% for the CNN and 10.0--10.3\% for prototype baselines; rulers 2.3\% vs.\ 3.9\% and 4.7--6.1\%). On predicted masks it flips the fewest of the prototype models (dark corners 5.7\% vs.\ 10.7--11.5\%, CNN 5.5\%; rulers 4.4\% vs.\ 7.7--9.7\%, CNN 6.7\%), with the smallest $|\Delta p|$ in both. The conclusions hold on expert masks alone, under $\pm5$-pixel errors and in every source.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
The two components play different roles. Lesion attraction changes what the network learns, making it more accurate, less dependent on artifacts and its prototypes more diverse. The constrained projection changes what the network shows: it guarantees the provenance of each explanation, which matters for presentation and audit, but is not itself a mechanism against shortcuts. Our evidence thus supports claims about prototype provenance and robustness to artifacts, not superior accuracy: the strongest mask-supervised ProtoPNet is as accurate and, across runs, as robust, and the black-box CNN is more accurate. Lesion masks are needed only in training and come from public expert masks of one source, so new dermoscopic data need no new annotation; as coarse $7\times7$ cell fractions, they tolerate boundary errors (Section~\ref{sec:maskrob}). Anchoring retains the lesion border: 9.3 of 20 prototypes of Ours are border cells (lesion fraction 0.5--0.95; baselines and mask-loss variants 1.7--5.0), so ink just outside the border draws some of its attention (Section~\ref{sec:cf}).

\textbf{Limitations.} (i)~A gap of about 3 AUC points to the black-box CNN remains; Correia \emph{et al.}~\cite{correia2023} found prototype models on par with CNNs, but on ISIC validation data after choosing among many configurations. (ii)~Predicted BCN20000 and MSK masks are validated only indirectly (held-out HAM10000, PH2, subsets), and the artifact detectors miss many artifacts, so attribution and real-artifact results concern detected ones only; insertion, background and prototype-audit tests need no detector. (iii)~Inserted artifacts are synthetic, may lie outside the training distribution and were not placed on the lesion, where anchoring cannot exclude them. Removing real artifacts, a null control and the test on unedited images mitigate this, but there Ours is significantly better only than PPN, PPN-FA and Ours-noLes, possibly partly due to lesion appearance. (iv)~The $7\times7$ grid limits explanation resolution, and class-activation and prototype maps differ in kind, so CNN-versus-prototype attribution comparisons are only indicative. (v)~External gains over the prototype baselines are only partly significant, and the original mask loss generalizes better. (vi)~We studied one backbone and one prototype architecture for a controlled comparison; both components apply to any prototype network with a projection step. Training variability rests on three seeds per configuration (seven for PPN, Correia and Ours; 12 paired networks for the projection), whose explanation metrics vary strongly. (vii)~No dermatologist rated the prototypes; clinical use needs prospective validation in other populations.
\section{Conclusion}
Against the artifact shortcuts of ISIC~2019, we anchored a prototype network to the lesion by lesion attraction on prototype attention and a projection restricted to lesion-dominated cells. In a same-backbone, leakage-controlled comparison, lesion attraction made ProtoPNet more accurate, also with the background masked, less swayed by swapped backgrounds and less prone to decision flips under all four inserted artifacts, across seven training runs and, for dark corners and rulers, at 90\% sensitivity. The projection alone fixed provenance without changing accuracy or robustness in 12 networks. The auxiliary artifact-suppression term had small, mixed effects. The strongest mask-supervised ProtoPNet was as accurate and, across runs, as robust and generalized better to Derm7pt, but kept all prototypes on lesion-dominated cells in one of seven runs, with more redundant prototypes; adding it to our model further weakened the real dark-corner shortcut. \textbf{Future work} will test lesion anchoring in other prototype architectures (e.g., PIP-Net~\cite{nauta2023pip}, via lesion attraction alone) and backbones, seek a combination with mask supervision that keeps its external generalization, use learned, higher-recall artifact detectors and have dermatologists rate the prototypes in a reader study. The counterfactual protocol can check other interpretable classifiers.
