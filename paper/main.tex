\begin{abstract}
Melanoma classifiers pick up artifacts such as dark corners, rulers and ink, and a prototype network's explanation (``this part looks like that prototype'') may point at the artifact. On ISIC~2019, dark corners and bright structures remain associated with melanoma after stratifying by source. Existing remedies supervise accuracy or activations, but in dermoscopy none controls \emph{which} training patches prototypes may become. We propose lesion-anchored prototype learning with two components. A lesion-attraction objective acts on each prototype's normalized spatial attention, and the projection step admits only cells that are at least half lesion and nearly free of detected artifacts. Every prototype thus comes from a lesion-dominated patch by construction, although its receptive field extends beyond it. The masks need no new annotation (Dice 0.94 on held-out expert masks), and inference needs none. In a same-backbone, leakage-audited comparison with lesion-grouped statistics, the model raises the AUC of ProtoPNet from 0.825 to 0.865 (0.859 without an auxiliary artifact-suppression term), not significantly different from the strongest mask-supervised ProtoPNet (0.867). When artifacts are inserted around bit-identical lesion pixels, lesion attraction roughly halves the decision-flip rate of ProtoPNet (dark corners: 10.9\%$\rightarrow$5.2\%). On average, the model also flips fewer decisions than any mask-loss baseline under dark corners and ink, with more stable explanations. The projection costs no accuracy and keeps every prototype on a lesion-dominated cell, which no mask loss achieved. Adding the original mask loss weakens the real dark-corner shortcut further, though that loss alone still generalizes better to Derm7pt. Code and logs: \url{https://github.com/YousefAMovahed/lesion-anchored-prototypes}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Convolutional networks can classify skin lesions as accurately as dermatologists~\cite{esteva2017}, yet they also learn \emph{shortcuts}~\cite{geirhos2020}: features that correlate with the label in the training data but are unrelated to the disease. In dermoscopy, surgical skin markings raise the melanoma probability of a deep classifier~\cite{winkler2019}, models perform far above chance even when the lesion is removed~\cite{bissoto2019,bissoto2020}, and dark corners, rulers, gel bubbles and ink are common and unevenly spread across sites~\cite{bissoto2022,yan2023,pewton2022}.

Rudin~\cite{rudin2019} argues that clinical use calls for models that are interpretable by design. The prototypical part network (ProtoPNet)~\cite{chen2019} compares image patches with learned prototypes, each projected onto a real training patch, so a decision reads ``this part looks like that part''. Nothing, however, prevents a prototype from being a piece of a ruler or a black corner. The shortcut then survives and is even presented as a reasonable explanation~\cite{hoffmann2021}; such prototypes have been seen in dermoscopy~\cite{correia2023,nauta2023pip}. \textbf{Research gap.} Prior work leaves three questions open (Table~\ref{tab:gap}). First, debiasing methods for dermoscopy~\cite{bevan2022,yan2023,pahde2023} improve robustness to artifacts, but their models are black boxes. Second, in prototype networks, mask supervision penalizes activation outside annotated regions~\cite{barnett2021,correia2023}, and human feedback removes or switches off unwanted prototypes~\cite{bontempelli2023,nauta2023pip}. None of these methods constrains the \emph{push} step, which decides which training patch a prototype finally \emph{is}, and feedback helps only once a user has spotted the problem. Third, artifact insertion has been used to test the \emph{decisions} of classifiers~\cite{nauta2022,nauta2023pip,pahde2023}, but, to our knowledge, not whether the \emph{explanations} of a prototype network stay on the lesion when artifacts are added or removed. \textbf{Contributions.} Our work addresses these gaps:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Lesion attraction on the normalized prototype attention.} An objective on each prototype's spatial attention pulls it toward the lesion without shrinking activations. It needs no new annotation and drives the gains in accuracy and decision robustness: without it, the test AUC drops from 0.865 to 0.830 ($p<0.001$) and flip rates under inserted dark corners, rulers and ink roughly double. The main model gains 4.0 AUC points over ProtoPNet (3.4 with the two core components alone) and does not differ significantly from the strongest mask-supervised ProtoPNet, the formulation of the closest prior work~\cite{correia2023}, whose explanations rely three times more on artifacts.
\item \textbf{Lesion- and artifact-constrained projection.} The push step admits only latent cells whose input patch is at least half lesion and at most 5\% detected artifact, so every prototype comes from such a cell \emph{by construction}. Loss-based supervision gives no such guarantee: no tested weight or formulation of the mask loss put all 20 prototypes on such cells in any run (on average 9--18). On six independently trained networks, each projected both ways, the step leaves the AUC unchanged and shifts neither flip rates nor explanation maps consistently; thresholds from 0.3 to 0.9 behave alike. It thus fixes which patch an explanation shows and barely affects the decisions. A foreground-restricted projection appeared concurrently as an appendix variant for natural images~\cite{lin2026}; the artifact exclusion, the combination with lesion attraction and the test against shortcuts are new.
\item \textbf{A counterfactual test of decisions \emph{and} explanations.} We insert four artifact types and remove real hair and ruler marks while keeping every lesion pixel bit-identical. With an inpainting-only null control and a complementary test on unedited images, we measure how far both the decision and the prototype explanation move.
\item \textbf{A leakage-controlled, mask-audited evaluation.} We group near-duplicates in addition to lesion identifiers and validate every mask source and the artifact detectors. Eight configurations, four further variants of the mask-loss baseline and its combination with our model all use the same backbone and three seeds; they are compared with lesion-grouped statistics and tested on three external sets.
\end{itemize}
The main model also keeps an auxiliary artifact-suppression term; it brought no significant gain on its own, so we report it as a largely negative result rather than a contribution.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Artifacts and shortcuts in dermoscopy.} Bissoto \emph{et al.} annotated seven artifact types, found that simple debiasing does not remove their bias~\cite{bissoto2020} and built artifact-based domain-generalization benchmarks~\cite{bissoto2022}. Removing dark corners barely changes accuracy but moves activations onto the lesion~\cite{pewton2022}, and pasted colored patches make a CNN call malignant lesions benign~\cite{nauta2022}. EPVT~\cite{yan2023} learns domain prompts for artifact groups, Bevan and Atapour-Abarghouei~\cite{bevan2022} unlearn artifact information adversarially, and Reveal-to-Revise (R2R)~\cite{pahde2023} finds band-aids, rulers and skin markers semi-automatically, corrects the model with RRR, CDEP or ClArC and tests it with pasted artifacts. All these models are black boxes.

\textbf{Prototype networks and explanation supervision.} The similarity a prototype network suggests can be misleading~\cite{hoffmann2021}, so explanations should be tested instead of taken at face value~\cite{adebayo2018}. RRR~\cite{ross2017} penalizes input gradients inside annotated irrelevant regions, and CDEP~\cite{rieger2020} penalizes contextual-decomposition importance, demonstrated on ISIC images with colored patches. IAIA-BL~\cite{barnett2021} adds a fine-annotation loss on prototype activation outside regions marked by radiologists. Closest to us, Correia \emph{et al.}~\cite{correia2023} apply this loss to melanoma-versus-nevus classification with lesion masks from a segmentation network; our PPN-FA baselines follow their approach, in its original formulation and in a weaker one (Section~\ref{sec:fa}). ProtoPDebug~\cite{bontempelli2023} and the medical study of PIP-Net~\cite{nauta2023pip} let a user mark confounded prototypes, which are then forgotten or switched off. None of these methods constrains the projection step or tests prototype explanations under artifact interventions. Concurrently, Lin and Wright~\cite{lin2026} study architectural prototype diversity on birds and faces, with a hinge loss on the background share of each prototype's activation and, in an appendix, a projection restricted to patches with more than 50\% foreground. We instead target shortcut artifacts, exclude them in the projection, need no new mask annotation and test with counterfactuals.

\subsection{Prototypical part network}
Our network builds on a DenseNet-121 backbone~\cite{huang2017}; Fig.~\ref{fig:masks}(b) gives an overview. Two $1\times1$ convolutions with a final sigmoid turn its output into a latent map $z\in[0,1]^{H\times W\times D}$ with $H=W=7$ and $D=128$. There are $m=20$ prototypes $p_j\in\mathbb{R}^D$, ten per class, and $c(j)$ denotes the class of prototype $j$. For every cell we compute the distance $d_j(h,w)=\lVert z_{hw}-p_j\rVert_2^2$ and convert it into a similarity $s_j(h,w)=\log\frac{d_j(h,w)+1}{d_j(h,w)+\epsilon}$ with $\epsilon=10^{-4}$. A prototype is as active as its best-matching cell, $a_j=\max_{h,w}s_j(h,w)$, and the logits are $\mathbf{W}\bm{a}$. Training minimizes
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}+\lambda_c\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{clst}}-\lambda_s\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sep}}+\lambda_l\Lles+\lambda_a\Lart ,
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}$ is a class-weighted cross-entropy and the cluster and separation costs measure the distance to the closest prototype of the own and of the other class~\cite{chen2019} ($\lambda_c=0.8$, $\lambda_s=0.08$ as originally). Our model combines both core components, lesion attraction $\Lles$ ($\lambda_l=1$) and the constrained projection (Section~\ref{sec:push}), with an auxiliary artifact-suppression term $\Lart$ ($\lambda_a=1$) whose ablation (Section~\ref{sec:lart}) shows small, mixed effects. All use the masks of Section~\ref{sec:masks}.

\subsection{Lesion attraction (core component)}
The masks receive the same geometric augmentation as the image, and average pooling to the $7\times7$ grid gives, for each cell, the fraction covered by lesion, $M^L(h,w)$, and by artifacts, $M^A(h,w)$. We describe where a prototype looks by its spatial attention $\alpha_j=\mathrm{softmax}_{h,w}(s_j/\tau)$ with $\tau=0.5$, a soft version of the max-pooling location that produces $a_j$. Each prototype is weighted by $\omega_j=a_j/\sum_k a_k$, treated as a constant, so that the prototypes the model actually uses count most. With $L_{ij}=\sum_{h,w}\alpha_{ij}M^L_i$, the attention mass of prototype $j$ on the lesion of image $i$,
\begin{equation}
\Lles=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}_L|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_L}\sum_{j:\,c(j)=y_i}\tilde{\omega}_{ij}\log\left(L_{ij}+\delta\right),
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{\omega}$ renormalizes $\omega$ over the prototypes of the true class $y_i$, $\delta=10^{-4}$, and $\mathcal{B}_L$ holds the batch images whose crop still shows lesion. The logarithm penalizes prototypes that almost ignore the lesion far more than those already looking at it. Since $\alpha_j$ sums to one, a larger lesion mass leaves less mass for everything else, artifacts included. The fine-annotation loss of IAIA-BL~\cite{barnett2021,correia2023} penalizes the magnitude of activation outside a mask, whereas $\Lles$ acts on the normalized attention and leaves the scale of the activations to the classification losses. Unlike the concurrent hinge on the background share of the activation~\cite{lin2026}, it rewards the lesion mass logarithmically and weights each prototype by its use.

\subsection{Lesion- and artifact-constrained projection (core component)}
\label{sec:push}
In the ``push'' step, ProtoPNet replaces each prototype with the closest latent patch from a training image of its class. This patch may lie on a ruler, a black corner or healthy skin even when the attention was well placed during training. We therefore admit only cells with $M^L\geq0.5$ and $M^A\leq0.05$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:masks}(b)). By construction, every prototype is then the latent vector of a cell whose $32\times32$ input patch is at least half lesion and contains at most 5\% detected artifact, and the ``that part'' of each explanation points to a patch dominated by lesion tissue. The constraint fixes where a prototype comes from; it cannot control every pixel that shapes the latent vector, because a cell also sees its surroundings (quantified in Section~\ref{sec:erf}). After the push, only $\mathbf{W}$ is trained, with backbone, add-on layers and batch-normalization statistics fixed, so each prototype stays identical to its source patch. Five last-layer epochs did not solve this convex problem for \emph{any} prototype model, so we re-optimize $\mathbf{W}$ to convergence on fixed training activations (weighted cross-entropy plus an $\ell_1$ penalty of $10^{-4}$ on negative-class connections~\cite{chen2019}; 3{,}000 full-batch Adam steps, cosine-annealed learning rate from 0.02), identically for every prototype model and without validation data. Results without it are reported as well.

\section{Masks: Provenance, Reliability and Leakage Control}
\label{sec:masks}
\subsection{Provenance and validation of the lesion masks}
Each image is tagged with the source of its mask. For the 10{,}015 HAM10000 images (39.5\% of ISIC~2019) we use the public expert segmentations of HAM10000~\cite{tschandl2018} unchanged. The 15{,}316 BCN20000~\cite{combalia2019} and MSK~\cite{codella2018} images have no public masks, so their masks are predicted: we train a DeepLabV3~\cite{chen2017deeplab} network with an ImageNet-initialized MobileNetV3-Large~\cite{howard2019} encoder at $256\times256$ (12 epochs, AdamW, one-cycle learning rate up to $5\times10^{-4}$, binary cross-entropy plus soft Dice, random flips and $90^\circ$ rotations) \emph{only} on the 8{,}163 HAM10000 images of our training split, which was fixed beforehand. The prediction is averaged with that of the flipped image, thresholded at 0.5, reduced to the largest connected component and hole-filled. Masks covering less than 0.5\% of the image (19 images, 0.08\%) are marked invalid and left out of mask-based losses and metrics. On the 1{,}852 HAM10000 images of the validation and test splits, which the segmentation network never saw, the predicted masks reach a mean Dice of 0.9445 against the expert masks (median 0.9672, mean Jaccard 0.9015). On the 200 expert-segmented PH2 images~\cite{mendonca2013}, taken in another clinic with another device, they reach a Dice of 0.918 without adaptation. Section~\ref{sec:maskrob} tests whether our conclusions depend on mask quality.

\subsection{Artifact masks}
Artifact masks come from fixed, label-free image operations at $256\times256$ and always exclude the dilated lesion. \emph{Dark corners} (DC) are dark regions (HSV value $<50$) touching the border and at least 8~px thick on average. \emph{Thin dark structures} (TS: hair, ruler ticks) are found with a $13\times13$ black-hat filter as in DullRazor~\cite{lee1997} plus an elongation test, \emph{bright structures} (SP: specular highlights, gel-bubble rims, also light hairs) with an $11\times11$ white top-hat filter, and \emph{ink or colored patches} (INK) as strongly saturated blue-to-violet pixels (Fig.~\ref{fig:masks}(a)). An image is flagged once a mask covers more than 1\%, 0.3\%, 0.05\% or 0.1\% of it. Compared with the public manual labels of Bissoto \emph{et al.}~\cite{bissoto2020}, available for 2{,}593 of our MSK images, the flags are precise but conservative (precision/recall: DC vs.\ dark corner 0.92/0.10, TS vs.\ hair or ruler 0.96/0.62, SP vs.\ gel bubble or border 0.86/0.35, INK vs.\ ink or patches 0.74/0.25); a visual check of 24 random DC and 24 random TS flags agrees (24 and 20 correct). Many artifacts are missed, above all faint dark corners (37\% manual vs.\ 4\% detected prevalence). ``Without artifacts'' therefore means ``without \emph{detected} artifacts'': an undetected artifact can enter a prototype cell, artifact shares count detected artifacts only, and the reference group of Section~\ref{sec:real} contains undetected ones, which pulls $A$ toward 0.5 for every model. In the 2{,}092 training images with manual image-level labels, only 1.9\% of the admissible cells lie on the outer ring (where dark corners lie) of an image with an undetected dark corner. For hair or rulers, gel and ink, which the labels do not localize, we can only give loose upper bounds (40\%, 43\%, 8\%). None of the 5 prototypes of Ours from these images is such a cell.

\subsection{How masks are used, and why they cannot leak}
Masks are used in four places only: in the training losses, in the constrained projection, in the attribution metrics and to place counterfactual edits. The classifier itself never receives a mask, so its test results do not depend on test masks. Leakage is prevented in several ways. The split was computed first, from image and lesion identifiers and image content alone, before any model, including the segmentation network, was trained. Images are grouped by lesion identifier and by near-duplicate pairs (Section~\ref{sec:data}), which can only remove leakage; an audit found no group, lesion identifier or image shared between sets, and all 342 near-identical pairs (thumbnail correlation $\geq0.97$) and all other verified near-duplicate pairs lie within one set. Supervision uses training data only: the segmentation network learns from the 8{,}163 training-split masks, mask-based losses and projection use training images, and the last-layer re-optimization uses training activations. Thresholds and checkpoints are chosen on validation, and the 1{,}852 expert masks of validation and test serve for evaluation alone. Neither the segmentation network nor the artifact detectors see a diagnosis, so the masks cannot carry label information. Derm7pt, PAD-UFES-20 and PH2 are used for evaluation only; removing external images flagged as possible near-duplicates of ISIC training images (8, 6 and 7) changes no external AUC by more than 0.004.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\subsection{Data and leakage-free split}
\label{sec:data}
The task is melanoma versus all other diagnoses on the 25{,}331 training images of ISIC~2019 (HAM10000~\cite{tschandl2018}, BCN20000~\cite{combalia2019}, MSK~\cite{codella2018}; 4{,}522 melanomas, 17.9\%), resized to $256\times256$. Unlike common practice, we keep the 2{,}084 MSK images without a lesion identifier. To avoid leakage, we group images by lesion identifier and by near-duplicate pairs (perceptual-hash distance $\leq8$ and thumbnail correlation $\geq0.9$ for full and central image). The 13{,}259 groups are split into ten stratified folds, one for testing and one for validation (about 81/10/9\% per source), with almost identical composition (Table~\ref{tab:dist}). PH2 has no diagnoses in our copy and only checks the segmentation.

\subsection{Models and training}
All configurations share backbone (ImageNet-pretrained DenseNet-121), input size ($224\times224$), data, split and schedule. \textbf{CNN}: backbone, global average pooling, linear layer (black box). \textbf{PPN-std}: ProtoPNet with standard augmentation (random crop, horizontal flip). \textbf{PPN}: ProtoPNet with strong augmentation (random resized crop, scale $[0.4,1]$; horizontal and vertical flips; $\pm20\%$ brightness, contrast, saturation), used by all following models. \textbf{PPN-FA}: PPN with the IAIA-BL fine-annotation loss~\cite{barnett2021} on our lesion masks as in Correia \emph{et al.}~\cite{correia2023}, with their $\lambda=10^{-3}$ but computed on the $7\times7$ maps and averaged over the batch, which weakens it (Section~\ref{sec:fa} tests the original formulations and larger weights). \textbf{Ours}: lesion attraction, constrained projection and the auxiliary $\Lart$ ($\lambda_l=\lambda_a=1$ in Eq.~(\ref{eq:loss})); the two core components alone are Ours-noArt. \textbf{Ablations}: without $\Lles$ (Ours-noLes), without $\Lart$ (Ours-noArt, the two core components only), with the standard projection (Ours-stdPush).

We train with Adam, batch size 64 and mixed precision: 5 epochs with frozen backbone (add-on layers and prototypes at $3\times10^{-3}$), 15 epochs of all layers (backbone $10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-3}$, learning rate divided by 10 every 5 epochs), then the projection and 5 last-layer epochs. The CNN is trained for the same 20 epochs and keeps its best validation checkpoint. Class weights are 0.608 and 2.81. Prototype models are always evaluated after the projection, with the last-layer epoch chosen on validation. Each configuration is run with seeds 0, 1 and 2 (about 30 minutes per run on an NVIDIA T4). Images and masks are stored at $256\times256$ and receive the same random crop and flips before bilinear resampling to the $224\times224$ input; test images are only resized. After pooling to the $7\times7$ grid, each cell covers a $32\times32$ input patch, and attribution maps are compared with masks at $224\times224$. All loss weights were fixed in advance and not tuned on test data.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
\textbf{Classification.} The threshold of each run is set on ISIC validation data (Youden index) and reused for every test set. AUC, balanced accuracy (BAcc), sensitivity and specificity are mean $\pm$ std over seeds; tests use seed ensembles (mean probability of the three seeds).

\textbf{Attribution.} For a prototype model, we upsample the similarity maps of the three most activated prototypes to $224\times224$; their top 5\% of pixels (the 95th-percentile region that ProtoPNet visualizes) form the hot region. For the CNN we use the class-activation map, which with a global-average-pooling head equals Grad-CAM~\cite{selvaraju2017} up to a positive factor~\cite{zhou2016}. We report the share of the hot region on the lesion and on artifacts, whether its maximum falls on an artifact (pointing), and the \emph{enrichment}, i.e., artifact share divided by artifact area (above 1: more attention than the area explains).

\textbf{Counterfactuals.} We modify each test image in four ways outside a 4-pixel margin around the lesion, adding a ruler along one border, 12--29 curved hairs, a dark vignette, or 2--4 purple ink arcs. Conversely, detected thin dark structures (real hair and ruler marks, 544 images) are erased by Telea inpainting~\cite{telea2004} outside a 2-pixel margin. As a null control, a region of the same shape is shifted onto artifact-free skin and inpainted. We verify that every lesion pixel stays bit-identical. We record the mean $|\Delta p|$ of the melanoma probability and the rate of flipped decisions. For the three prototypes most active on the original image, we also record the Spearman correlation $\rho$ between the top-1 maps before and after the edit, and the \emph{capture}, the part of the new hot region on inserted pixels.

\textbf{Statistics.} The 2{,}320 test images form 1{,}234 groups of the split (a lesion with its near-duplicates; 471 with several images), so we resample whole groups. Ensemble AUCs get 95\% CIs from 1{,}000 group resamples and are compared by a paired bootstrap with 2{,}000 (two-sided $p$). Per-image metrics, averaged over seeds and then within groups, are compared by the paired Wilcoxon test. Derm7pt (one image per case) uses the paired DeLong test~\cite{delong1988,sun2014}, PAD-UFES-20 a bootstrap over lesions. The primary family, corrected with Holm's method, holds the 16 tests behind the main claims: Ours against each of PPN-std, PPN, PPN-FA and the strongest mask-loss variant of Section~\ref{sec:fa} on AUC, artifact share and flip rate under inserted dark corners and rulers. All other tests are secondary, uncorrected and exploratory. Image-level tests, which ignore the grouping, agree unless noted. All tests treat the trained models as fixed and capture the uncertainty of the test sample; variation over training runs is shown by the std over the three seeds and checked at the seed level in Sections~\ref{sec:cf} and~\ref{sec:abl}.

\section{Results}
We compare five main models (CNN, PPN-std, PPN, PPN-FA, Ours) unless stated otherwise; ablations, mask-loss variants and the artifact-suppression term follow in Sections~\ref{sec:abl}--\ref{sec:lart}. Bold: best of the four main prototype models.

\subsection{Artifacts are associated with melanoma}

Source could explain this, as 47\% of BCN but 5\% of HAM images have dark corners and BCN has the highest melanoma rate. For dark corners the OR exceeds 1 in every source (BCN 1.41, HAM 3.10, MSK 2.24), for bright structures in BCN and HAM (2.11, 1.92) but not in MSK (0.88). The strength differs between sources (Breslow--Day $p<10^{-9}$), so pooled odds ratios are averages. Thin dark structures and ink show no association.

\subsection{Classification}
\label{sec:cls}
On the ISIC test set, Ours reaches the highest AUC among the prototype models ($0.865\pm0.006$, ensemble $0.884$; Table~\ref{tab:cls}). Its ensemble exceeds those of PPN-std, PPN and PPN-FA by 0.022, 0.018 and 0.017 (95\% CI $[0.009,0.036]$, $[0.004,0.032]$, $[0.005,0.031]$; Holm-adjusted $p=0.012$, $0.042$, $0.029$), and its seeds vary less (std 0.006 vs.\ 0.040 for PPN). As a descriptive check, each of the three Ours seeds beats each of the nine baseline seeds (worst 0.8592 vs.\ best 0.8526; on Derm7pt-dermoscopic 0.7405 vs.\ 0.7343). Of seven Ours seeds (Section~\ref{sec:abl}; 0.863$\pm$0.012, range 0.842--0.880), one falls below the best baseline seed. At the validation threshold, Ours trades sensitivity for specificity. The black-box CNN remains more accurate (0.912 vs.\ 0.884, $p=0.009$), a price of about 3 AUC points for interpretability (4.6 for PPN). Without the last-layer re-optimization (five-epoch last layer, epoch chosen on validation) all prototype models score lower, but Ours stays best (0.846$\pm$0.007; baselines 0.765--0.797, ablations 0.787--0.841), significantly so against the baselines ($p\leq0.001$), and it loses less than they do (0.019 vs.\ 0.039--0.079).

\subsection{Where the models look}
\label{sec:attr}
The black-box CNN is the model most drawn to artifacts (Table~\ref{tab:attr}, Fig.~\ref{fig:expl}(a)): in images with artifacts, 23.1\% of its hot region lies on them, 1.5 times their area share. The prototype models look at artifacts less than their area suggests, Ours least (4.8$\pm$1.5\% of the hot region, maximum on an artifact in 1.3$\pm$0.5\% of images; $\pm$: std over seeds), with the largest lesion fraction (58.9\%). In every Ours run, all 20 prototypes lie on lesion cells ($M^L\geq0.5$; Fig.~\ref{fig:erf}), whereas PPN-FA projects on average 10.0 onto lesion and 2.0 onto artifact cells. Most attention on artifacts falls on dark corners, where Ours has the smallest share (Table~\ref{tab:attr2}). On the test sample, Ours differs significantly from each other main model in lesion share, artifact share, enrichment and pointing (Wilcoxon on lesion-group means, $p\leq5.6\times10^{-4}$; artifact shares in the primary family: Holm-adjusted $p\leq2.2\times10^{-5}$). Across training runs, these values overlap with those of PPN and PPN-std (Section~\ref{sec:cf}). On Derm7pt-dermoscopic, Ours again has the lowest artifact share, enrichment and pointing rate ($p\leq8.6\times10^{-3}$), but the three prototype baselines place more of their hot region on the lesion ($p\leq1.2\times10^{-4}$; Table~\ref{tab:attr2}).

\textbf{Receptive field of the prototypes.}\label{sec:erf} We measure the effective receptive field (ERF) of each prototype's source cell as the input-gradient magnitude of its latent vector on the source image (eight random sign projections). For every model, only 8--12\% of its mass lies inside the cell, and half of it spreads over 8--11\% of the image. For Ours, $69\pm3$\% lies on the lesion (10th percentile over prototypes 42\%), against 48--53\% for PPN-std, PPN and PPN-FA (10th percentiles 11--21\%), and 2.5\% on detected artifacts (4.3\%, 2.7\%, 7.3\%). This comes from where prototypes are taken; the field itself is not narrower. The constraint selects source images whose lesion covers 54\% of the image (baselines 40\%), and relative to the lesion area every model concentrates its ERF on the lesion by a median factor of only 1.2--1.3. Ours-stdPush drops to 47\%, Ours-noLes and Ours-noArt keep 71--72\%. For 13\% of the prototypes of Ours (baselines 22--28\%), the ERF draws less on the lesion than its area would, so anchoring covers the projected cell and most, not all, of its receptive field (Fig.~\ref{fig:erf}).

\subsection{Counterfactual robustness}
\label{sec:cf}
Table~\ref{tab:cf} summarizes the interventions (2{,}063--2{,}318 modified images per insertion type). Adding dark corners \emph{increases} the melanoma probability of every model (mean signed change $+0.041$ CNN, $+0.048$ PPN, $+0.020$ Ours), matching the association in Table~\ref{tab:bias}; the models have absorbed the shortcut, Ours the least. Ours roughly halves the flip rate of PPN for dark corners (10.9\%$\rightarrow$5.2\%) and rulers (7.5\%$\rightarrow$3.6\%) and reduces it for ink (9.5\%$\rightarrow$5.6\%) and hair (13.9\%$\rightarrow$9.2\%); it also flips fewer decisions than the CNN for all four insertions. Against every other main model these reductions are significant for all four insertions (Wilcoxon on lesion-group means, $p\leq4.9\times10^{-8}$ against the prototype baselines, Holm-adjusted $p\leq3.9\times10^{-7}$ for dark corners and rulers; $p\leq0.008$ against the CNN), as is the smaller $|\Delta p|$ against PPN ($p<10^{-27}$) and, except for hair ($p=0.09$), the CNN ($p\leq2.3\times10^{-4}$). Removing real hair and ruler marks changes more decisions than the null control for every model (Ours 4.4\% vs.\ 1.6\%), so inpainting alone cannot explain this sensitivity.

The explanations of Ours also move the least (Fig.~\ref{fig:expl}(b)). After dark corners are added, its top-1 map keeps $\rho=0.92$ (PPN 0.68), and only 3.4\% of its hot region (per seed 0.2--7.7\%) moves onto the inserted pixels (PPN 6.1\%, PPN-FA 11.4\%; $p<10^{-84}$). Ink is the exception. It is drawn just outside the lesion border, where anchored prototypes respond, and 1.8\% of the hot region of Ours falls on it vs.\ 1.0\% for PPN, though Ours still flips fewer decisions.

At the seed level, including the four further Ours networks of Section~\ref{sec:abl} (seven seeds), every Ours seed flips fewer decisions under inserted dark corners and ink than every seed of PPN-std, PPN and PPN-FA (3.3--7.5\% vs.\ 7.6--15.4\%; 4.4--8.0\% vs.\ 8.0--11.2\%, i.e., at most 184 vs.\ at least 185 of 2{,}301 images). Every Ours seed also keeps a more stable map than every seed of PPN-std and PPN-FA ($\rho$ 0.85--0.93 vs.\ $\leq$0.77); $\rho$ overlaps with one PPN seed (0.87) and the mask-loss variants ($\leq$0.90). The artifact share (3.0--8.9\%) lies below every CNN seed ($\geq$20.7\%) but overlaps with the prototype baselines and the Correia formulation, and the captured share overlaps for all models (Ours 0.2--7.7\%, baselines 0.1--15.4\%).

\subsection{Shortcuts in unedited images}
\label{sec:real}
As a complementary test without editing, we ask whether artifacts present in the test images raise the melanoma score of \emph{benign} lesions. We measure it by the threshold-free probability $A$ of Table~\ref{tab:real} ($A=0.5$: no effect). On ISIC, dark corners double the false-positive rate of PPN-FA (16\%$\rightarrow$33\%) but raise that of Ours only from 17\% to 20\%. The effect $A$ of Ours (0.57) is significantly smaller than that of PPN, PPN-FA and Ours-noLes (0.61--0.66; $p<0.001$) and not significantly different from that of PPN-std and the CNN ($p\geq0.77$). Our weak mask loss thus leaves the real dark-corner shortcut in place, lesion attraction keeps it small, and the original mask-loss formulations reduce it as well (Section~\ref{sec:fa}). The same holds for bright structures, where Ours has the smallest effect of the prototype models (0.59 vs.\ 0.60--0.62; $p\leq0.038$ against PPN-std and PPN-FA), and most clearly for dark border regions in the clinical Derm7pt photographs (0.69 vs.\ 0.78--0.82; DeLong $p\leq1.4\times10^{-6}$). Although the CNN attends to dark corners most, its effect is not larger than that of Ours.

\subsection{Ablation of the two core components}
\label{sec:abl}
\textbf{Lesion attraction} drives the accuracy gain. Without it (Ours-noLes), the test AUC falls to 0.830 ($p<0.001$), the artifact share of the hot region more than doubles (4.8\%$\rightarrow$10.6\%), and the flip rates for dark corners, rulers and ink roughly double. Ours-noLes still uses the constrained projection, so all its prototypes lie on lesion cells; a correct projection is not enough without anchoring the attention during training.

\textbf{The constrained projection} determines what the prototypes \emph{are} (Ours-stdPush: 7.7 of 20 on lesion cells). It does not change the accuracy (ensemble difference within $[-0.006,0.005]$, 95\% CI) or, significantly, the flip rates (dark corners 4.5\% vs.\ 5.2\%; $p\geq0.13$); decision robustness thus comes from lesion attraction. The map differences of Ours-stdPush in Tables~\ref{tab:attr} and~\ref{tab:cf} (artifact share 8.6\% vs.\ 4.8\%, captured share 7.5\% vs.\ 3.4\%) are not a systematic effect of the projection. We projected six independently trained networks both ways: seeds 0 and 2, where Ours and Ours-stdPush share one network (identical test latents; seed 1 diverged), and four new seeds whose trained prototypes we stored before the projection. In every network the constraint moved the prototypes onto lesion cells (6--11 $\rightarrow$ 20 of 20; exact Wilcoxon $p=0.031$, the smallest possible with six pairs). The AUC stayed the same (mean difference $+0.0001$), and flip rates, artifact share, captured share and $\rho$ showed no consistent change (differences of both signs, $p\geq0.22$).

We also varied the thresholds on the four new networks, again projecting from their stored trained prototypes. Lesion thresholds of 0.3--0.9 (artifact fraction $\leq$0.05) and artifact limits of 0, 0.2 or none (lesion fraction $\geq$0.5) put 14.8--20 of 20 prototypes on lesion cells (mean lesion fraction of the source cells 0.78--0.99 vs.\ 0.47 without constraint). The AUC (0.861--0.863), dark-corner flips (5.8--6.1\%), artifact share (5.1--5.5\%) and $\rho$ (0.885--0.886) stay at the standard projection's values (0.861, 5.8\%, 5.2\%, 0.886). Only the strictest threshold (0.9) removes most border cells (2.3 vs.\ 8.5 at 0.5).

\subsection{Strength of the mask-loss baseline}
\label{sec:fa}
IAIA-BL~\cite{barnett2021} and Correia \emph{et al.}~\cite{correia2023} compute the fine-annotation loss at $224\times224$ and sum it over the batch. With the same $\lambda=10^{-3}$, their loss is about three orders of magnitude stronger than in PPN-FA (factors 32 and 64; Correia \emph{et al.} penalize own-class prototypes only). We therefore trained both original formulations and PPN-FA with $\lambda=10^{-2}$ and $10^{-1}$, three seeds each (Table~\ref{tab:fa}; secondary tests). The ISIC AUC of the Correia formulation does not differ significantly from that of Ours (ensemble difference $-0.001$, 95\% CI $[-0.012,0.011]$), and it generalizes better (Derm7pt-dermoscopic 0.771 vs.\ 0.749, Derm7pt-clinical 0.702 vs.\ 0.657, PAD-UFES-20 0.699 vs.\ 0.647; $p\leq0.028$). Yet only 13 of its 20 prototypes are lesion cells, and its hot region lies three times more on artifacts (14.1\% vs.\ 4.8\%, $p<10^{-29}$; seeds 8.1--24.6\% vs.\ 3.0--8.9\%). In the primary family, Ours remains significantly better than this formulation on artifact share and dark-corner flips (Holm $p<10^{-28}$, $p=0.029$), but not on AUC or ruler flips. The IAIA-BL formulation keeps its hot region on the lesion (83\%) and off artifacts (3.9\%) better than Ours ($p<0.002$) but is less accurate (0.845; ensembles $p=0.057$) and leaves 2 of 20 prototypes off lesion cells. Every variant flips more decisions than Ours under inserted dark corners (6.2--12.2\% vs.\ 5.2\%) and ink (6.6--10.7\% vs.\ 5.6\%; $p\leq0.02$), although per seed the original formulations, and for dark corners also $\lambda=10^{-1}$, overlap with Ours. For rulers only the original formulations ($p\geq0.052$), and for hair only the Correia one ($p=0.37$), do not differ significantly from Ours, and none keeps its explanation as stable (Section~\ref{sec:cf}). The real dark-corner effect of both original formulations does not differ significantly from that of Ours ($A$ 0.57--0.58 vs.\ 0.57, $p\geq0.26$), unlike our weak version (0.66). Mask supervision thus shapes where the network looks and helps it generalize. Anchoring fixes what the prototypes are and keeps decisions stable, so the two are complementary.

Adding the Correia loss to our model (Ours+Correia) gives the highest ISIC AUC of all prototype models (0.871$\pm$0.004; $p\geq0.32$ against Ours and Correia). Its maps are more lesion-focused than those of Ours (lesion share 73\% vs.\ 59\%, seeds 59--85\% vs.\ 49--69\%; artifact share 4.3\% vs.\ 4.8\%, captured share 2.2\% vs.\ 3.4\%; $p<10^{-7}$), its real dark-corner effect is the smallest of all models ($A=0.53$; $p<0.001$ against both), and its flip rates are similar ($p\geq0.26$; hair 7.9\% vs.\ 9.2\%, $p=0.02$). It does not inherit the Derm7pt advantage of the mask loss (0.736 and 0.675 vs.\ 0.771 and 0.702; DeLong $p\leq0.016$), but it has the highest PAD-UFES-20 AUC of all models (0.737, CNN 0.721; $p\leq0.001$ against both). Its maps are less stable than those of Ours ($\rho$ 0.87 vs.\ 0.92).

\subsection{Artifact suppression: a largely negative result}
\label{sec:lart}
The auxiliary term penalizes attention on artifacts directly. With $A_{ij}=\sum_{h,w}\alpha_{ij}M^A_i$, the attention mass of prototype $j$ on the artifacts of image $i$,
\begin{equation}
\Lart=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\omega_{ij}A_{ij}.
\end{equation}
Removing it (Ours-noArt) does not change the accuracy significantly, either on ISIC (ensemble difference 0.007, 95\% CI $[-0.004,0.018]$) or on any external set ($p\geq0.058$). Several counterfactual measures are even better without it (e.g., $|\Delta p|$ 0.061 vs.\ 0.081 for added hair, $p<10^{-40}$), and the attribution changes little (artifact share 4.2\% vs.\ 4.8\%, $p=0.36$; pointing 1.8\% vs.\ 1.3\%, $p=5.7\times10^{-5}$). The explanation gains come from the two core components alone: against PPN-std, PPN and PPN-FA, Ours-noArt has more hot region on the lesion and less on artifacts and flips fewer decisions under all four insertions ($p\leq5.2\times10^{-4}$). Its AUC gain over them (0.010--0.016), however, is not significant with lesion groups resampled ($p=0.073$--$0.17$; image-level DeLong $p=0.005$ against PPN-std), so the significant accuracy gain belongs to the main model. $\Lart$ mainly weakens the response to real bright structures and to dark border regions in clinical images ($A$ 0.59 vs.\ 0.62 and 0.69 vs.\ 0.75; $p<0.001$, Table~\ref{tab:real}); otherwise its effect is small. We therefore keep $\Lart$ as an auxiliary term but do not claim it as a contribution.

\subsection{Robustness to mask quality}
\label{sec:maskrob}
Predicted masks could bias the results if a model looked ``on the lesion'' only as judged by an imperfect mask. We therefore split the ISIC test set by mask provenance and perturb the lesion masks (Table~\ref{tab:maskrob}).

On the HAM10000 test images, which have expert masks, Ours places 53.4\% of its hot region on the lesion (prototype baselines 41.6--44.6\%, CNN 44.4\%). This advantage is \emph{larger} than on the whole test set (+8.8 vs.\ +2.7 points over the best baseline), so it is not produced by errors of the predicted masks, and its artifact scores are the lowest there too. On the predicted-mask images, Ours has the lowest artifact share (5.6\% vs.\ 7.9--27.7\%), although PPN places slightly more on the lesion (63.5$\pm$1.5\% vs.\ 62.4$\pm$2.6\%).

Eroding or dilating every lesion mask by 5~pixels shifts the lesion fractions of all models alike and keeps the order of the five main models (Ours first); artifact share, pointing and enrichment change by at most 0.011. The provenance guarantee itself depends on the mask border: with masks eroded by 5 pixels, on average 17 of the 20 prototypes of Ours remain on cells with $\geq$50\% lesion (lowest 0.36), all 20 with dilated masks. Ours is also the most accurate of the four main prototype models in every source, with expert (HAM10000) and with predicted training masks (BCN20000, MSK; Table~\ref{tab:maskrob}).

A predicted mask that is too small could let inserted artifacts touch the true lesion. On the expert-mask subset, where the protected region is exact, Ours flips the fewest decisions (dark corners 4.4\% vs.\ 7.1\% for the CNN and 10.0--10.3\% for the prototype baselines; rulers 2.3\% vs.\ 3.9\% and 4.7--6.1\%). On the predicted-mask subset it flips the fewest among the prototype models (dark corners 5.7\% vs.\ 10.7--11.5\%, CNN 5.5\%; rulers 4.4\% vs.\ 7.7--9.7\%, CNN 6.7\%), with the smallest $|\Delta p|$ in both. The conclusions thus hold on expert masks alone, under $\pm5$-pixel mask errors and in every source.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
The two components play different roles. Lesion attraction changes what the network learns and makes it both more accurate and less dependent on artifacts. The constrained projection changes what the network shows to the user: it guarantees the provenance of each explanation, which matters for presentation and audit, but it is not itself a mechanism against shortcuts. Our evidence therefore supports claims about prototype provenance and robustness to artifacts; superior accuracy is not shown, as our AUC does not differ significantly from that of the strongest mask-supervised ProtoPNet and stays below that of the black-box CNN. Lesion masks are needed only in training and come from public expert segmentations of one source, so a new dermoscopic dataset needs no new annotation. Because they enter only as coarse $7\times7$ cell fractions, our conclusions tolerate boundary errors (Section~\ref{sec:maskrob}). Anchoring retains the lesion border: 9.3 of 20 prototypes of Ours are border cells (lesion fraction 0.5--0.95; baselines and mask-loss variants 1.7--5.0), which is why ink just outside the border draws some of its attention (Section~\ref{sec:cf}).

\textbf{Limitations.} (i)~A gap of about 3 AUC points to the black-box CNN remains. Correia \emph{et al.}~\cite{correia2023} found prototype models on par with CNNs, but on ISIC validation data and after choosing the best of many configurations. (ii)~Predicted BCN20000 and MSK masks are validated only indirectly (held-out HAM10000 images, PH2, subset analysis), and the artifact detectors miss many artifacts (Section~\ref{sec:masks}). Our evidence therefore concerns detected and inserted artifacts only. (iii)~Inserted artifacts are synthetic and may lie outside the training distribution. The removal of real artifacts, a null control and the test on unedited images mitigate this, but on unedited images the advantage of Ours is significant only against PPN, PPN-FA and Ours-noLes, and these effects may be partly confounded by lesion appearance. (iv)~The $7\times7$ grid limits the spatial resolution of the explanations. Class-activation and prototype maps differ in kind, so attribution comparisons between the CNN and the prototype models are only indicative. (v)~External gains over the prototype baselines are only partly significant (Section~\ref{sec:cls}), and the original mask loss generalizes better (Section~\ref{sec:fa}). (vi)~We studied one backbone and one prototype architecture, as a controlled comparison requires, although both components apply to any prototype network with a projection step. The loss weights were fixed a priori, and training variability rests on three seeds per configuration (seven for Ours, six paired networks for the projection), between which explanation metrics vary strongly (Section~\ref{sec:cf}). (vii)~No dermatologist rated the prototypes, and clinical readiness would need prospective validation in other populations, which a retrospective study cannot provide.
\section{Conclusion}
Classifiers trained on ISIC~2019 exploit artifact shortcuts. We anchored a prototype network to the lesion without new annotation, by lesion attraction on the prototype attention and a projection restricted to lesion-dominated cells. In our same-backbone, leakage-controlled comparison, it reached an ISIC AUC not significantly different from that of the strongest mask-supervised ProtoPNet and kept all prototypes on lesion-dominated cells by design. Under artifacts inserted around an unchanged lesion, it flipped significantly fewer decisions than every baseline for dark corners and ink and never significantly more for any insertion, with more stable explanations on average. Each of its seven seeds also flipped fewer decisions under dark corners than any seed of the ProtoPNet baselines. Lesion attraction drove accuracy and robustness, and the projection fixed provenance without changing either; the auxiliary artifact-suppression term had only small, mixed effects. Adding the original mask loss further weakened the real dark-corner shortcut, while that loss alone generalized better to Derm7pt. \textbf{Future work} will test lesion anchoring in other prototype architectures (e.g., PIP-Net~\cite{nauta2023pip}) and backbones and seek a combination with mask supervision that also keeps its better external generalization. It will also replace the conservative artifact detectors by learned, higher-recall ones and evaluate the prototypes with dermatologists in a reader study. The same counterfactual protocol can check other interpretable classifiers.
